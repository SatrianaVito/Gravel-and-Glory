Episode 1: "If Buffum Can Do It"
Pilot teleplay. Calumet, Michigan, to Dumbarton, Scotland, winter 1982. Bowie Oil Rally Team.

COLD OPEN

EXT. KEWEENAW STATE FOREST — BRIGGS GRADE — DUSK

November. The sky is the color of an old nickel. Birch and hemlock crowd a gravel road barely wider than a hay wagon. The first snow of the season has dusted the crown of the road and nowhere else.

Silence. A crow on a stump. Breath-fog from nobody.

Then, from far off, a sound: a four-cylinder engine screaming at the top of its lungs. A gear change. Screaming again. Closer. The crow reconsiders its life choices and leaves.

SUPER: "COPPER COUNTRY PRO RALLY. KEWEENAW PENINSULA, MICHIGAN. NOVEMBER 1982."

A DATSUN 510 comes over a crest with all four wheels in the air, a bar of four Cibié lamps blazing like a locomotive. Pale yellow where it isn't mud-brown. Hand-lettered on the doors: KOSKI SERVICE & REPAIR, CALUMET, MICH. A big crooked #7. It lands, bottoms out with a crunch that echoes through the trees, sprays a rooster tail of gravel into the ditch, and is gone around a bend.

The crow comes back to the stump. Thinks better of it again.

INT. DATSUN 510 (MOVING) — CONTINUOUS

Stripped bare. Welded roll cage. A Halda Tripmaster clicking over tenths of a mile. Everything shakes: the dash, the cage, the teeth of the two men inside.

DEAN KOSKI (29) drives. Square jaw, mustache that he is extremely proud of, a grin that has gotten him out of three speeding tickets and into two marriages he did not follow through on. Open-face helmet with a hand-painted checkered stripe.

Beside him, TOMMY KOSKI (26). Wire-rimmed glasses taped at one hinge. Clipboard strapped to his thigh. He reads pace notes by a map light the size of a thimble, his voice flat and calm like a man reading a grocery list during an earthquake.

TOMMY
Hundred. Four left over crest. Don't cut. Stump inside.

DEAN
Heard ya.

TOMMY
You heard me last year too. Don't cut.

The car crests. Dean cuts.

BANG. Something hard hits the underside of the car like God knocking on the floorboards.

TOMMY (CONT'D)
That was the stump.

DEAN
That was a pinecone.

TOMMY
That was a stump the size of a Buick, Dean. Fifty. Six right into three left, tightens. Caution, ditch outside.

Dean flicks the car, the tail swings out, gravel hammering the wheel wells like hail on a tin roof. He catches it with a quarter-turn of opposite lock, feet dancing.

DEAN
How far back are we?

TOMMY
(checking a stopwatch taped to the dash)
Voss finished twelve minutes ago. We need to take eleven point four seconds off him on this stage to win. On nine miles.

DEAN
Eleven seconds. That's nothing. That's a sneeze.

TOMMY
It's a real long sneeze, Dean. Two hundred. Five left.

EXT. BRIGGS GRADE — STAGE FINISH — SPECTATOR AREA — CONTINUOUS

A clearing near the flying finish. A dozen pickups parked nose-in to the woods, headlights on. Woodsmoke from a burn barrel. Forty locals in blaze orange, Carhartt, snowmobile suits, and Detroit Lions stocking caps. Somebody has a thermos of coffee that is at least forty percent brandy.

At the front of the crowd, stamping his swampers in the snow: EINO "IKE" KOSKI (58). Barrel-chested, silver crewcut, hands like two catcher's mitts that have been dipped in 10W-30 for three decades. Red-and-black buffalo plaid Mackinaw. A can of Stroh's in one fist, a stopwatch in the other.

Next to him, BEV LAHTI (56), who runs the Copper Kettle Diner, bundled in a fake-fur hood with a clipboard of her own. And ARNIE PERTTULA (70s), leaning out the window of his Ford F-100, holding a CB mic.

ARNIE
(into CB)
Breaker one-nine, this is the Moose Hunter, anybody seen the Koski boys go by the Hautamaki bridge, come back.

CB VOICE (V.O.)
(crackle)
Moose Hunter, yeah, they went by sideways, over.

IKE
Sideways is good. Sideways is fast.

BEV
Last year you said sideways was how you end up in a tree.

IKE
Last year Dean was sideways the wrong direction.

Across the clearing: GARY VOSS (34), Denver, feathered hair under a Voss Mazda satin jacket, leaning on his white Mazda RX-7, #1 on the doors, VOSS MAZDA OF DENVER in big red script. His co-driver sips coffee. Voss catches Ike's eye and taps his watch, grinning.

VOSS
(calling over)
Eleven seconds, Ike! Tell your boys to go home and stick to snowmobiles!

IKE
(calling back, cheerfully)
Gary, when my boys get here I'm gonna tell 'em you said that, and then I'm gonna hold your jacket while they pound you into the gravel.

The crowd laughs. Voss laughs too, but less.

BEV
(low)
Ike. You're shaking.

IKE
It's twenty degrees, Bev.

BEV
You're shaking in the happy way.

He doesn't deny it. He checks his stopwatch. Checks it again.

INT. DATSUN 510 (MOVING) — CONTINUOUS

Blasting down a straight, the engine bouncing off the rev limiter. Dean is humming. Actually humming.

TOMMY
Are you humming?

DEAN
It's Seger.

TOMMY
Stop humming Seger and listen. Three hundred into long six left, don't lift. Don't lift.

DEAN
I never lift.

TOMMY
You lifted at Marquette.

DEAN
There was a moose at Marquette!

TOMMY
It was a mailbox.

DEAN
It was a mailbox SHAPED like a moose, Tommy!

Dean doesn't lift. The car drifts through the long left at a speed that turns Tommy's knuckles the color of skim milk, the outside wheels skimming the snow bank, snow blasting over the windshield. Wipers slap. Clear. Tommy's voice doesn't change.

TOMMY
Good. Good. Hundred fifty. Water splash. Keep it straight. It's deep.

DEAN
How deep?

TOMMY
I don't know, Dean, I didn't bring a yardstick. Deep.

EXT. HAUTAMAKI CREEK CROSSING — CONTINUOUS

A frozen-rimmed ford where the road dips through a creek. The Datsun hits it at sixty. A wall of brown water explodes over the car, over the lights, over a pair of astonished MARSHALS in a Jeep who are now very wet.

MARSHAL
(soaked, deadpan)
Well, that's a Koski.

INT. DATSUN 510 (MOVING) — CONTINUOUS

The engine COUGHS. Sputters. The lamps flicker. Dean pumps the throttle.

DEAN
C'mon. C'mon, Loretta. Don't do this to me, baby.

TOMMY
Distributor's wet.

DEAN
I know the distributor's wet!

The engine catches, misses, catches again. Coughing, then roaring. Dean whoops.

DEAN (CONT'D)
That's my girl!

TOMMY
(checking watch)
That cost us three seconds. Maybe four.

DEAN
Then I'll find five.

TOMMY
Where? In your mustache? We're not gonna make it up.

DEAN
You know what John Buffum would do right now?

TOMMY
Win! He'd win, Dean, because he's John Buffum and he's got an Audi with four-wheel drive and a budget bigger than Calumet!

DEAN
He'd believe, Tommy. He'd believe.

TOMMY
Oh my God. Five right. Five right! Don't believe, TURN!

Dean turns. Hard. The Datsun swings, clips a snowbank, carves a perfect channel through it, and fires out the other side.

TOMMY (CONT'D)
DEER!

A white-tailed DOE stands in the middle of the road, frozen in the lights, looking personally offended.

DEAN
Where's that in the notes?!

TOMMY
It's a DEER, Dean, they don't come in the NOTES!

Dean flicks the car left, then right, a Scandinavian flick his father taught him in a church parking lot when he was eleven. The Datsun pendulums around the deer with maybe eighteen inches to spare. The doe watches them go, chewing.

DEAN
(grinning, breathing hard)
I love this sport.

TOMMY
(breathing harder)
I'm gonna throw up in my helmet.

DEAN
Not in the car. Loretta's got enough problems.

TOMMY
Two hundred. Crest into big jump. Caution. CAUTION. That's Dad's Hump.

A beat. Both brothers go quiet. Dean's grin changes. Not smaller. Different.

DEAN
How fast did Dad take it in '61?

TOMMY
Too fast. He ended up in a tree. Mom had to pick him up in the Rambler.

DEAN
Then we take it a little faster than too fast.

TOMMY
That's not a number, Dean, that's not a real number—

Dean shifts up.

EXT. BRIGGS GRADE — "DAD'S HUMP" — CONTINUOUS

A crest in the road with a sharp drop behind it, lit by headlights of spectator trucks. Thirty people stand well back. A hand-painted sign nailed to a birch: IKE'S HUMP, EST. 1961. SLOW DOWN DUMMY.

The Datsun comes over it flat out. It FLIES. Five feet off the ground. Ten. Longer than anything should fly that was built in Japan to carry a family of four to church. The crowd's cheer rises with it.

In the air, in SLOW MOTION: we see inside. Dean, eyes wide, mouth open, joyful. Tommy, clipboard flat against his chest, lips moving, reading the next note to a car that isn't touching the planet.

TOMMY (CONT'D)
(slow, in the air)
...land... hundred... flying finish...

It lands. Not gracefully. The rear bumper takes a bite of Michigan. Sparks. It keeps going.

EXT. BRIGGS GRADE — STAGE FINISH — CONTINUOUS

The flying-finish line: two orange signs and a timing marshal with a clipboard. Ike, Bev and the crowd hear the engine before they see the lights.

ARNIE
(into CB)
He's through the Hump! He's through the Hump, the damn fool!

IKE
(to himself, stopwatch clicking)
Come on. Come on, Deanie.

The Datsun blasts past the finish sign at a hundred and five. Ike clicks his stopwatch. Looks at it. His lips move. He does math. He does it again.

BEV
Well?

IKE
I got it by two.

BEV
Two what?

IKE
Seconds, Bev. Two seconds.

VOSS
(across the clearing)
Bullshit! Your watch is bullshit, Ike! Your watch is from a cereal box!

IKE
My watch is from Sears, Gary, and it's never been wrong in its life!

EXT. BRIGGS GRADE — STOP CONTROL — MOMENTS LATER

The Datsun grinds to a stop at the stop control, steam and smoke curling off it, one headlamp pointed at the sky, something dragging underneath. A TEEN MARSHAL (16) in a purple snowmobile suit, SHERRI, leans to the window with a clipboard.

Tommy hands her the time card through the window. She writes. Tommy watches her write like a man watching a surgeon. She hands it back. He reads it. Then he looks at a second sheet: Voss's stage times, scrawled in pencil.

DEAN
Well?

Tommy does the arithmetic silently. Twice. Behind his glasses, his eyes go very, very wide.

DEAN (CONT'D)
Tommy. Say it.

TOMMY
(quietly)
One point nine.

DEAN
One point nine what?

TOMMY
We won the Copper Country by one point nine seconds.

A long beat. Two brothers, sitting in a wrecked car in the dark woods, staring at a scrap of paper.

Then they SCREAM. Both of them. Helmets still on. Dean pounds the steering wheel. Tommy pounds the roof. The horn goes off. Sherri, startled, drops her pencil.

DEAN
ONE POINT NINE! ONE POINT NINE, BABY!

TOMMY
I TOLD YOU TO LIFT!

DEAN
I DIDN'T LIFT!

TOMMY
I KNOW! THAT'S WHY WE WON!

Headlights bounce up the road behind them. Pickups. People running. Leading the pack, Mackinaw flapping, running like a man twenty years younger and forty pounds lighter: Ike.

He rips Dean's door open, hauls him out by the collar of his fire suit, and bearhugs him off his feet. Tommy clambers out the other side and Ike grabs him too, one son in each arm, the Stroh's somehow still in his hand, spilling beer down Tommy's back.

IKE
My boys! MY BOYS!

DEAN
Dad, you're spilling—

IKE
I don't CARE! I got more! Arnie! Get the cooler!

Arnie is already dragging a Styrofoam cooler across the gravel. Bev arrives, tears in her eyes, fake fur hood askew, and kisses Tommy on the cheek, leaving a lipstick smear.

BEV
Your mother'd be screamin' her head off right now.

TOMMY
(emotional, covering it)
Mom never screamed.

BEV
She screamed at every single one of your father's rallies. She just did it in the car with the windows up.

Ike holds his sons' faces in his huge hands. His eyes are wet and he doesn't care who sees.

IKE
You went flat over the Hump.

DEAN
Yeah.

IKE
You maniac. You beautiful maniac.
(to Tommy)
And you let him.

TOMMY
Nobody lets Dean do anything, Dad.

IKE
(laughing)
That's God's truth.

Across the road, Voss is at his RX-7, snarling at his co-driver, who is holding the results sheet and shrugging.

DEAN
(shouting over)
Hey Gary! Snowmobiles, huh?!

Voss flips him the bird. The whole crowd flips it back, cheerfully, in unison. Somebody lights a road flare. Somebody else sets off a bottle rocket. It goes into a tree.

Tommy slips away, just for a second, and looks back at the Datsun. At the dent in the bumper. At the stump-mark on the skid plate. He runs a hand along the roof like it's a horse that just won the Derby. He looks at the driver's seat. Just a glance. It lingers a half-second too long.

Then Dean's arm lands around his neck and drags him back into the crowd.

DEAN
C'mere, Professor! You're gettin' a beer shower!

Beer showers everywhere. The crow on the stump, back again, observes all of it with contempt.

ACT ONE

EXT. CALUMET, MICHIGAN — FIFTH STREET — MORNING

A copper-mining town that peaked in 1910 and has been politely declining ever since. Red sandstone storefronts. A church steeple. Snowbanks already knee-high on the curbs. A bedsheet banner hangs across the street between two lampposts, spray-painted by someone with enthusiasm but no plan: CONGRATS DEAN & TOMMY!! COPER COUNTRY CHAMPS!!

Somebody has since added a second P with a marker. It's crooked.

A county snowplow rumbles past and honks twice. From a window above the hardware store, an OLD WOMAN in a housecoat bangs a pot with a wooden spoon.

INT. COPPER KETTLE DINER — MORNING

Wood paneling, vinyl booths, a Hamm's beer clock with the moving waterfall. Pasties under a glass dome. Radio behind the counter tuned to WMPL out of Hancock, farm report giving way to Juice Newton. The windows are fogged from the inside.

The place is packed. Every seat taken by men in Carhartt and women in Lutheran church sweaters. Morning coffee is a sacrament here.

On the counter, next to the pie case, in a place of honor usually reserved for the Christmas nativity: a TROPHY. Cheap brass-plated plastic on a walnut base. COPPER COUNTRY PRO RALLY 1982, 1ST OVERALL. Someone has already put a pasty next to it, as if feeding it.

Ike sits at the counter on a stool that groans under him, holding court. Bev pours coffee down the line.

IKE
—so they come outta the water splash, right, and the distributor's soaked, the thing's coughin' like Arnie after a cigar—

ARNIE
(from a booth)
Hey.

IKE
—and Dean, instead of backin' off like a normal human being, he puts his foot DOWN. And you know what Tommy says? Tommy says, calm as you please, like he's orderin' a pound of bologna, "That cost us three seconds." Three seconds! In the dark! Upside down half the time!

BEV
They weren't upside down, Ike.

IKE
In spirit they were upside down, Bev.

Laughter down the counter. A logger, SWEDE HAAPALA (40s), leans in.

SWEDE
Heard Voss is filin' a protest.

IKE
Voss can file it where the sun don't shine and the snow don't melt. Protest what? That he's slow?

SWEDE
Says Dean cut a corner and went off the road.

IKE
He didn't go off the road. The road just moved over a little.

Bigger laughs. The bell over the door JINGLES. Dean and Tommy come in, stamping snow off their boots. Dean in a letterman jacket that no longer quite zips over thirty years of pasties. Tommy in a sensible parka with the hood fur matted.

The diner APPLAUDS. Actual applause. Somebody whistles. PASTOR HELMI (60s), in a clerical collar, raises his coffee mug. Dean spreads his arms wide, soaking it up.

DEAN
Thank you, thank you. Autographs are a dollar, pictures are two, kisses are free for ladies over seventy.

MRS. KANGAS
(80s, from a booth, without looking up)
I'll take one.

Dean crosses and kisses Mrs. Kangas on the cheek. She blushes like a schoolgirl and goes back to her oatmeal.

Tommy slides onto a stool beside his father. Bev sets down a coffee before he's asked. Also a cinnamon roll the size of a hubcap.

BEV
On the house, champ.

TOMMY
Thanks, Bev.

IKE
(clapping Tommy's shoulder so hard his coffee jumps)
There he is. The brains of the operation.

DEAN
(sliding onto the other stool)
Hey. I'm the brains too.

IKE
You're the foot. Tommy's the brains. I'm the wallet.

TOMMY
You haven't been the wallet since '79. I've been the wallet.

IKE
(beat, to the diner)
He's also the brains of the wallet.

Tommy pulls a small spiral notebook from his parka and flips it open. Pencil columns. Neat as a ledger.

TOMMY
So. Prize money for first overall was two thousand. Minus entry fee, three-twenty. Minus fuel, four-ten. Minus the tires we shredded, six hundred eighty. Minus the rear bumper, the skid plate, the lower control arm and whatever the stump did to the diff—

DEAN
Tommy.

TOMMY
—which Dad says is about seven hundred in parts—

DEAN
Tommy, we're in public.

TOMMY
—we cleared negative one hundred and ten dollars.

A pause at the counter.

SWEDE
You won and you lost money?

TOMMY
Welcome to rallying, Swede.

IKE
(beaming, not bothered at all)
Negative a hundred and ten dollars and the best night of my life! Bev, put their breakfast on my tab.

BEV
Ike, your tab's longer than the Mackinac Bridge.

IKE
Then add a little on the end, it'll never notice.

Bev rolls her eyes but writes it down. There's something between them, easy and warm and not quite said.

Dean has picked up a copy of the Daily Mining Gazette from the counter. Page three: a grainy photo of the Datsun mid-air. The headline: LOCAL BROTHERS FLY TO RALLY WIN.

DEAN
Page three? We're page three? What's on page one?

TOMMY
(reading over his shoulder)
Deer season numbers.

DEAN
Of course it is.

IKE
In the U.P., son, nothin' beats deer. Not even you.

On the TV mounted in the corner, a sports bulletin: grainy footage of an Audi Quattro, white with red and black stripes, sliding through snow somewhere in Europe. The Sports anchor's voice is barely audible over the diner noise. Tommy's eyes flick up to it. Stay there.

TV ANCHOR (V.O.)
...and in Europe, the German automaker Audi says its four-wheel-drive Quattro will be the car to beat in next season's World Rally Championship...

Dean notices Tommy watching. Follows his eyes. The two brothers look at each other. Something passes between them. Neither says anything.

IKE
(oblivious, to the diner)
—and THEN he goes over the Hump flat! My Hump! Flat!

INT. KOSKI SERVICE & REPAIR — NIGHT

A two-bay cinderblock garage on the edge of town, the kind that fixes everything from John Deeres to church vans. Sodium light buzzing. Hand-painted sign out front, dusted in snow: KOSKI SERVICE & REPAIR. TOWING. WELDING. NO CHECKS FROM TROLLS.

(A "troll," in the U.P., is anyone who lives below the Mackinac Bridge.)

Inside: a PARTY. The Datsun sits on the lift in Bay One like a guest of honor, wounded and proud, draped with a string of Christmas lights. Somebody's taped a paper crown to the roof.

Forty people. Stroh's, Old Milwaukee, Blatz. Crockpots of Swedish meatballs. A card table covered in bars, the Upper Midwest's dessert of record. A boombox on a tool chest plays Bob Seger's "Night Moves" and three middle-aged men sing along with deep, off-key sincerity.

Ike, in a clean flannel, is showing a group of kids the damage on the skid plate with a trouble light.

IKE
See that? That's where your Uncle Dean hit a stump. You know why he hit it?

KID
'Cause he's a dummy?

IKE
(delighted)
'Cause he's a dummy! That's right! Here's a quarter.

Across the garage, Dean is holding court with the SUOMALAINEN SISTERS, KARI and LIISA (20s, twins, big hair), doing a demonstration of the jump using a Stroh's can as a car and his other hand as the ground.

DEAN
—so we're flying, right, and it's quiet, it's real quiet, you can hear Tommy reading the notes—

KARI
What was he saying?

DEAN
He was saying "we're gonna die, we're gonna die"—

TOMMY
(passing with a crockpot)
I was saying "hundred, flying finish."

DEAN
In a way that sounded like "we're gonna die."

The twins laugh. Tommy sets the crockpot down, looks around at the party, the lights, his father's laughing face. Happy. But there's a restlessness in him.

He slips through a door marked OFFICE.

INT. KOSKI SERVICE & REPAIR — OFFICE — CONTINUOUS

Tiny. Wood-paneled. A metal desk buried in invoices. A rotary phone. A Snap-on calendar featuring a woman holding a torque wrench in a way that has nothing to do with torque. Framed photos on the wall: a young Ike beside a 1959 Saab 93 caked with mud; Ike and a pretty dark-haired woman, MARY, laughing on a frozen lake; the boys as kids sitting on the hood of a Ford Falcon rally car.

And one photo, half-hidden behind a Pennzoil clock: young Ike and a lanky young man in a cowboy hat, arms around each other, beside a wrecked, burned-out car. Written on the photo in ballpoint: ALBERTA '64. NEVER AGAIN.

Tommy doesn't look at that one. He goes straight for the bottom drawer of the desk, pulls out an accordion file stuffed fat. He sets it on the desk. Opens it.

Inside: AUTOSPORT magazines, months old, curled and dog-eared, shipped from England. Clippings. Maps of Europe. A Michelin guide. Carbon copies of letters. A pad covered with columns of numbers, heading underlined twice: WRC 1983. EST. BUDGET.

He runs a finger down the columns. Mouths numbers. Rubs his eyes.

The door opens. Dean leans in, beer in hand.

DEAN
There you are. The twins want to know if you—
(sees the file)
What's that?

TOMMY
(too fast)
Nothin'. Invoices.

DEAN
Invoices don't come in Autosport, Tommy.

Tommy sighs. Dean closes the door behind him, and the party noise drops to a muffle. He picks up a magazine. On the cover: an Audi Quattro, three wheels in the air, flames licking from the exhaust. MOUTON NEARLY TAKES THE TITLE. He flips another: a stubby, wide-hipped, bright-liveried Renault 5 Turbo on a mountain road. Another: a Lancia 037, low and wedge-shaped and Italian and beautiful.

DEAN (CONT'D)
(slowly)
How long you been collecting these?

TOMMY
Since Mom died.

That lands. Dean sits on the edge of the desk.

TOMMY (CONT'D)
I needed something to think about at night that wasn't... that. So I thought about this. What it'd cost. What we'd need. Where we'd stay. How you ship a car across the ocean. It's stupid.

DEAN
It's not stupid.

TOMMY
It's a little stupid.

DEAN
(grinning)
Okay, it's a little stupid.
(beat)
You want to know something stupider?

Dean pulls out his wallet. From behind his driver's license, he produces a newspaper clipping, folded so many times it's soft as cloth. He unfolds it carefully. A photo of a bearded man in a driving suit beside a rally car. The headline: VERMONT'S BUFFUM TAKES ON EUROPE'S BEST.

DEAN (CONT'D)
Carried that since '78. John Buffum. American. Went over there. Ran with 'em. Didn't embarrass himself. Didn't embarrass us.

TOMMY
Dean, Buffum's got factory backing, he's got—

DEAN
He's got two arms, two legs, and a steering wheel. Same as me.

TOMMY
He's got a brain that works.

DEAN
Eh, that's what you're for.

He holds up the clipping next to the budget pad.

DEAN (CONT'D)
If John Buffum can do it, we can too.

TOMMY
You can't just say that like it's a law of physics.

DEAN
It is a law of physics. Newton's fourth law. If Buffum can do it, the Koskis can do it. It's in all the textbooks.

TOMMY
(laughing despite himself)
You failed physics.

DEAN
I failed physics 'cause Mr. Heikkinen was a communist.

TOMMY
He was a Methodist.

DEAN
Same thing.

Tommy laughs. Then stops. Looks at the numbers again. The laugh is gone.

TOMMY
Dean. Look at me. We've never raced anywhere but here. Michigan, Ontario, Washington State once. We've never seen a mountain road that's actually on a mountain. Monte Carlo's got ice and tarmac on the same stage. Portugal's got a quarter million people standing IN the road. The Acropolis'll eat a transmission for breakfast. Those guys, Mikkola, Blomqvist, Mouton, Röhrl—

DEAN
Röhrl's goin' to Lancia next year.

TOMMY
I know Röhrl's going to Lancia, Dean, I'm the one who told you! Those guys have been doing this since they were twelve. They've got factories. Hundreds of mechanics. Helicopters with spare parts. We've got a Datsun named after a Loretta Lynn song and Dad's floor jack.

DEAN
And we won.

TOMMY
We won the Copper Country.

DEAN
We won Sno*Drift. We won Budweiser Forest. We came second at POR with a busted half-shaft. Tommy, we've won four of our last five. Every guy in this country with a factory deal, we've beat 'em. Who's left?

Tommy doesn't answer.

DEAN (CONT'D)
That's right. Nobody. There's nobody left here. So either we keep winning two grand and losing a hundred and ten dollars till we're Dad's age, or we go find out how good we actually are.

TOMMY
(quiet)
And if we're not?

DEAN
Then we'll know. And we'll come home and fix tractors and tell lies at the Copper Kettle about the time we went to Monte Carlo.

A long pause. The party thumps through the wall.

TOMMY
(finally)
Three hundred and forty thousand dollars.

DEAN
What?

TOMMY
Full season, lean. Car, spares, shipping, entries, hotels, fuel, tires, a service crew, recce cars. Three forty. That's if nothing breaks. Things break.

Dean whistles low.

DEAN
That's a lot of pasties.

TOMMY
That's the whole town's worth of pasties, Dean. For ten years.

DEAN
(standing)
Okay. So we ask Dad.

TOMMY
Dad doesn't have three hundred and forty thousand dollars. Dad has a two-bay garage and a Pontiac with a coat hanger for an antenna.

DEAN
Dad knows people.

TOMMY
Dad knows Arnie.

DEAN
Dad knows people you don't know about. C'mon.

He grabs the accordion file. Tommy grabs his arm.

TOMMY
Not tonight. He's happy tonight.

DEAN
That's exactly why tonight.

INT. KOSKI SERVICE & REPAIR — BAY TWO — LATER

The party's thinning. The boombox is down to a tired John Cougar tape. A few guys asleep on folding chairs. Bev is wrapping bars in tinfoil.

Ike lies on a creeper under a church van in Bay Two, a trouble light hooked on the frame, humming. Just his boots sticking out. Even at a party, he can't not work.

Dean and Tommy stand over his boots, the accordion file between them.

DEAN
Dad.

IKE (O.S.)
(from under the van)
Pastor Helmi's van's got a leak in the rear main. I told him it's 'cause he's been sinning.

DEAN
Dad, come out. We gotta talk to you.

IKE (O.S.)
If one of you got a girl pregnant, it's Dean, and I'm not coming out.

TOMMY
Nobody got anybody pregnant.

IKE (O.S.)
Then it can wait for the rear main.

DEAN
It's about rallying.

A pause. The creeper rolls out. Ike lies on his back looking up at them, oil on his cheek, wrench on his chest.

IKE
What about rallying?

Dean looks at Tommy. Tommy opens the file. Takes a breath.

TOMMY
We want to run the World Rally Championship next year. The full season. Starting with Monte Carlo in January.

Ike looks at Tommy. Looks at Dean. Then he laughs. A big, warm, belly laugh that echoes off the cinderblock.

IKE
Oh, that's good. That's real good. Monte Carlo! Bev! Bev, get over here, the boys are going to Monte Carlo!

BEV
(from across the garage)
Oh good, bring me back a prince!

Ike's still laughing. He sits up on the creeper. Then he looks at his sons' faces. Really looks. Neither of them is laughing.

The laugh drains out of him slowly, like air out of a tire.

IKE
(quiet)
You're serious.

DEAN
Dead serious.

IKE
(to Tommy)
You're serious?

TOMMY
I've got a budget.

IKE
(looking at the file)
Of course you've got a budget.
(beat)
How long've you had a budget?

TOMMY
Three years.

Ike absorbs that. He stands, knees cracking, and wipes his hands slowly on a red shop rag. He looks at the Datsun on the lift, wrapped in Christmas lights. He looks at the photos through the office door.

IKE
How much?

TOMMY
Three hundred and forty thousand. Lean.

IKE
(no reaction at all)
Uh-huh.

DEAN
Dad, if Buffum can do it—

IKE
Don't.
(beat)
Don't you Buffum me at midnight in my own garage.

Silence. Ike folds the rag. Unfolds it. Folds it again.

IKE (CONT'D)
Go home. Sleep it off. Ask me again when you're sober.

DEAN
We're sober.

IKE
Then ask me again when I'm sober.

He lies back down on the creeper and rolls under the van. The trouble light swings. Dean opens his mouth. Tommy puts a hand on his arm and shakes his head.

The brothers leave. The garage door rattles behind them.

Under the van, Ike stares up at the rusty frame of Pastor Helmi's Dodge. Not working. Just staring. His lips move. He's doing math.

IKE (CONT'D)
(whispering)
Three hundred and forty thousand... Jesus, Mary and Joseph.

Bev's shoes appear beside the creeper.

BEV (O.S.)
You okay down there?

IKE
They want to go to Europe, Bev.

A pause.

BEV (O.S.)
Well. You did too, once.

IKE
I know.
(beat)
That's what I'm scared of.

ACT TWO

EXT. KOSKI HOUSE — DRIVEWAY — DAWN

A white farmhouse with a sagging porch and a satellite dish the size of a wading pool, the most expensive thing on the property. Snow falling lightly. Ike's '74 Pontiac Catalina idles in the driveway, exhaust curling blue. The coat-hanger antenna is real.

Ike leans on the horn. Long. Longer.

An upstairs window flies up. Dean, hair wrecked, in a Red Wings T-shirt.

DEAN
It's six in the morning!

IKE
I'm sober. Get in the car. Both of ya.

INT. PONTIAC CATALINA (MOVING) — DAWN

The heater blasts and does nothing. Dean in the front, Tommy in the back, both in parkas over pajamas, both clutching Thermos cups of coffee Ike made that could strip paint. The radio plays polka softly: a Finnish hour out of Hancock. Nobody talks.

TOMMY
Where are we going?

IKE
You'll see.

DEAN
Is this the part where you drive us out to the woods and shoot us?

IKE
Don't tempt me.

EXT. LAKE VIEW CEMETERY — DAWN

A small cemetery on a hill, iron fence, birches. The whole world is white and blue. Beyond the hill, far off, the gray line of Lake Superior.

Ike's boots crunch through the snow between headstones. The boys follow. He stops at a simple granite stone, snow brushed off the top by someone recently.

MARY ANNE KOSKI. 1929–1979. BELOVED.

A plastic poinsettia sits frozen in a coffee can at its base. Ike squats with a grunt and brushes the snow off the letters with his bare hand.

IKE
Mornin', Mare.
(beat)
The boys won the Copper Country. You probably heard. Dean went over my Hump flat. Tommy let him. I cried like a little girl in front of the whole damn town.

The boys stand behind him. Tommy takes his glasses off and cleans them on his parka. Dean looks at the lake.

IKE (CONT'D)
And now they want to go to Europe.

He stands. Doesn't turn around. Talks to the stone.

IKE (CONT'D)
You remember what you said to me in '64. When I came back from Alberta. You remember.

A long silence. Wind in the birches.

DEAN
What'd she say?

IKE
(turning, finally)
She said, "Eino, next time you want to go chasing something that can kill you, you talk to me first." So.
(beat)
I'm talking to her first.

He looks at his sons. His eyes are clear and very steady.

IKE (CONT'D)
Walk with me.

EXT. LAKE SUPERIOR — McLAIN BEACH — MORNING

The lake in November. Gray and vast and unforgiving. Ice forming in plates at the shoreline, grinding. Big waves out past it. Wind that goes straight through Gore-Tex, which doesn't exist yet anyway.

The three Koskis walk along the frozen sand. Ike with his hands jammed deep in his Mackinaw.

IKE
First thing. I want you to hear this. I want you to hear it and remember it, no matter what else I say.
(stops, faces them)
I have never, in my life, been prouder of anybody than I was of you two last night. Not myself. Not anybody. I've seen every driver that ever came through this peninsula. Buffum. Carlson. The Canadians. The Swedes they fly in. I've seen 'em all. And you two are the best I've ever seen. Better than me. Better than me at my best, and I was pretty goddamn good.

DEAN
Dad—

IKE
Let me finish. I don't make speeches. I get one a year and this is it.
(beat)
You're good enough. That's not the question. I know you're good enough. The question is whether the thing is good enough for you. Whether it gives you back what you give it.

TOMMY
What do you mean?

Ike looks out at the lake.

IKE
I ran POR when it was a time-speed-distance rally, before it was ever a Pro event. Slide rules and a Curta calculator and a stopwatch. I ran the Shell 4000 across Canada, three thousand miles, Montreal to Vancouver. I ran in snow so deep you had to follow the telephone poles. I thought I was the toughest son of a bitch in North America.
(beat)
And I was. In North America.

He picks up a flat stone, turns it over in his fingers.

IKE (CONT'D)
What they run over there, I never touched it. Never came close. Those roads in the Alps, they got drops a thousand feet with a little stone wall no higher than your knee. In Portugal, the people stand in the road till the car's ten feet away and then they part like the Red Sea. Somebody's gonna die in Portugal one of these years, you mark my words. In Corsica there's ten thousand corners and every one of them's got a rock face on one side and the sea on the other. The Acropolis, the dust is so thick you drive blind at a hundred miles an hour following the sound of the car ahead.

DEAN
We can learn all that.

IKE
Sure you can. That's not what scares me.
(beat)
Those factory teams. Audi, Lancia, Opel. They spend more on one rally than this whole county makes in a year. They got fifty mechanics, sixty. They got helicopters flying over the stages with spare parts hanging underneath. They got engineers with slide rules and Frenchmen with stopwatches. And you know what they don't got?

TOMMY
What?

IKE
Any reason at all to be nice to three hillbillies from Calumet.
(skips the stone; it hits ice and stops dead)
I don't know that world. That's what I'm telling you. I know gravel and snow and Datsuns and Fords and how to fix a busted diff in a ditch at two in the morning with a coat hanger and a prayer. I don't know how you get a car through French customs. I don't know how you talk to an Italian. I don't know where you buy a tire in Greece. Nobody at the Copper Kettle knows. I'd be sending you into a place I can't protect you.

A long silence. The ice grinds.

DEAN
(quiet)
You're saying no.

IKE
(sharp)
Did I say no? Did you hear the word "no" come out of my mouth?

DEAN
It kinda sounded like—

IKE
It sounded like a man who's scared, Dean. Scared and proud aren't opposites. I'm both. I'm standing here being both at the same time and it's giving me heartburn.

Tommy steps forward.

TOMMY
Dad. What do you need? To say yes. What would it take?

Ike looks at his younger son for a long moment. Of course it's Tommy who asks.

IKE
Terms. My terms. You don't like 'em, we go home, you win Sno*Drift again in February, and nobody ever brings this up.

DEAN
Name 'em.

Ike holds up one thick finger.

IKE
One. Safety's mine. The cage, the seats, the belts, the fire system, the helmets. Everything. I get the final word, and my word's final. I don't care if it costs us a second a mile. I don't care if the Scotsman or the Italian or the Pope says otherwise.

TOMMY
What Scotsman?

IKE
Figure of speech.
(second finger)
Two. Tommy runs the money. Every dime. And when the money runs out, we come home. We don't borrow. We don't mortgage this house. We don't mortgage the shop. Your mother paid off that house with her Christmas cookie money one tin at a time and I'm not putting it up against a rally in Finland.

TOMMY
Agreed. Absolutely.

IKE
(third finger)
Three. Nobody drives angry. Not after a fight, not after a bad stage, not after some French son of a bitch says something about your mustache. You drive angry, you get out of the car.

DEAN
(grumbling)
What's wrong with my mustache?

IKE
Nothing. That's why they'll make fun of it. Europeans are jealous people.
(fourth finger)
Four.
(beat; this is the one)
If I say we pull out, we pull out. Any stage. Any rally. The whole season. No argument. No "one more stage, Dad." No Buffum.

Dean shifts. He doesn't like this one. Tommy sees him not liking it.

DEAN
Dad, what if we're leading?

IKE
Then you'll be leading when we pull out.

DEAN
That's not—

IKE
Dean.

DEAN
What if you're wrong?

IKE
Then I'm wrong and you're alive to be mad at me about it.

The wind whips. Dean looks at the lake. His jaw works. Tommy watches him.

TOMMY
Dean.

DEAN
(finally)
...Fine. Four. Fine.

IKE
Say it.

DEAN
If you say we pull out, we pull out.

IKE
(to Tommy)
And you.

TOMMY
If you say we pull out, we pull out.

Ike nods. Slowly. Then he puts his arm around each of them and pulls them in, three men in parkas on a frozen beach. He speaks quietly into the space between their heads.

IKE
Then we're goin' to Monte Carlo.

Dean lets out a whoop that echoes off the lake. Tommy laughs, shaky, and wipes his eyes on his sleeve and pretends it's the wind.

DEAN
We're goin' to Monte Carlo! Holy wah!

TOMMY
We don't have any money.

DEAN
Details!

IKE
(already walking back to the car)
Money's the easy part.

TOMMY
(following)
Money is NEVER the easy part. Money is the only part! Where are we getting three hundred and forty thousand dollars?

IKE
(not turning around)
I'm gonna call somebody I haven't talked to in eighteen years.

DEAN
Who?

IKE
A crazy Texan.

The brothers exchange a look behind his back. Neither has any idea what he's talking about.

INT. KOSKI SERVICE & REPAIR — OFFICE — LATER THAT MORNING

Ike sits at the desk. A beige rotary phone before him. Coffee. A small address book with a rubber band around it, the pages yellow and soft. He flips to B. A single entry, the ink faded: HARLAN BOWIE. A phone number, crossed out. Another number below, crossed out. A third, newer, written in different ink. Midland, Texas.

Through the office window, Dean and Tommy are pretending to work on the Datsun and very obviously eavesdropping. Dean holds a wrench to nothing.

Ike stares at the number. Glances at the photo behind the Pennzoil clock: ALBERTA '64. NEVER AGAIN.

He picks up the receiver. Dials. The rotary clicks back, number by number, agonizingly slow.

OPERATOR (V.O.)
Long distance, what number please?

INTERCUT WITH:

INT. BOWIE PETROLEUM TOWER — HARLAN'S OFFICE — MIDLAND, TEXAS — SAME

Twenty-two stories up, the tallest building for a hundred miles in any direction. Floor-to-ceiling glass looking out over pumpjacks bobbing on flat brown earth to the edge of the world. The office is enormous. A longhorn skull over the desk. A mounted bobcat. An oil painting of the owner on horseback, much slimmer than in life. A wet bar. A putting green.

Behind a desk the size of a Buick, feet up, cowboy boots of ostrich skin, a white Stetson on his head indoors: HARLAN T. BOWIE (57). Big man. Big smile. Big voice. A gold bolo tie shaped like the state of Texas. He is on another phone, yelling cheerfully.

HARLAN
—I don't care what the Saudis say, Hank, the Saudis don't own the Permian Basin, I own the Permian Basin, and my momma owned it before me! You tell 'em that! You tell 'em Harlan Bowie says howdy and go to hell!

He slams the phone down. Laughs. A second line buzzes. His SECRETARY, LUANN (40s, hair architecturally significant), speaks through the intercom.

LUANN (V.O.)
Mr. Bowie, long distance, a Mr. Koski from Michigan?

Harlan freezes. The smile doesn't vanish; it goes still, like a photograph.

HARLAN
What'd you say?

LUANN (V.O.)
A Mr. Eino Koski. Says you'd know him. Should I tell him you're in a meeting?

Harlan takes his feet off the desk. Slowly. Takes his hat off. Sets it down. Picks up the phone.

HARLAN
(soft, for him)
Put him through, Luann.

A click.

HARLAN (CONT'D)
...Eino?

IKE
Harlan.

A long pause on both ends. Two old men, fifteen hundred miles apart, both sitting very still.

HARLAN
(and then, exploding)
Ike Koski, you sorry sonuvabitch! Eighteen years! Eighteen years and not a Christmas card! I thought you was dead! I thought a moose ate you!

IKE
(smiling despite himself)
Moose don't eat people, Harlan.

HARLAN
A Michigan moose'll eat anything! How the hell are ya? How's Mary? You still with that pretty girl who was too good for ya?

A beat. Ike looks at the photo of Mary on the frozen lake.

IKE
Mary passed. Three years ago. Cancer.

On Harlan's end: silence. His big face collapses. He closes his eyes.

HARLAN
(quiet)
Aw, hell, Ike. Aw, hell. I'm sorry. I'm real sorry. She was... she was somethin'. She sent me a letter after Alberta. You know that? She wrote me, while I was in the hospital in Calgary.

IKE
(surprised)
I didn't know that.

HARLAN
Told me if I ever got you in a race car again she'd drive down to Texas and shoot me herself.

IKE
(laughing, wet)
Yeah. That sounds like her.

HARLAN
I believed her, too. That's why I never called.

Ike wipes his nose with the back of his wrist.

IKE
Listen, Harlan. I got two boys.

HARLAN
I remember. Little towheads. One of 'em bit me.

IKE
That was Dean. He's twenty-nine now. Tommy's twenty-six.

HARLAN
Lord. Where'd the time go.

IKE
They drive, Harlan. Rally. Dean drives, Tommy calls the notes. They just won the Copper Country. They've won four of the last five big SCCA events in the country. And now they want to go to Europe. The World Championship. The whole season.

On Harlan's end, a slow, enormous grin spreads.

HARLAN
Well, now.

IKE
I need a sponsor.

HARLAN
How much?

Ike covers the receiver, leans out of the office.

IKE
(to the boys)
How much?

TOMMY
(instantly)
Three hundred and forty thousand.

IKE
(back into phone)
Three forty. Lean.

A pause. Harlan swivels his chair to face the window, the endless pumpjacks.

HARLAN
Ike. How long we known each other?

IKE
Since '58.

HARLAN
And in all that time, have you ever once asked me for a single thing?

IKE
No.

HARLAN
Not even after Alberta. Not even when I told you I owed you my life and anything you ever wanted, just say the word.

IKE
(quiet)
I didn't want anything.

HARLAN
I know. It's been eatin' at me for eighteen years.
(beat)
Get your boys on a plane. I want to look 'em in the eye. I don't give money to people over the phone, Ike. Not even you. Texas don't do business that way.

IKE
When?

HARLAN
How's Friday? I'll have my man pick you up in Midland. Bring warm-weather clothes. You Yankees always dress like you're fixin' to fight a bear.

IKE
Harlan... thank you.

HARLAN
Don't thank me. I haven't said yes yet.
(grinning)
But I'm gonna.

He hangs up. Sits there. The grin fades. He looks out at his empire. He opens a desk drawer and takes out a photograph: the same one from Ike's office, the two young men in front of the burned-out car. ALBERTA '64. His hand isn't steady.

HARLAN (CONT'D)
(to himself)
Well, Mary. I'm sorry. I'm gonna put him back in one.

INT. KOSKI SERVICE & REPAIR — BAY ONE — SAME

Ike walks out of the office. The boys drop all pretense.

DEAN
Well?!

IKE
We're going to Texas.

TOMMY
Who is Harlan Bowie?

IKE
An old friend.

DEAN
What happened in Alberta?

Ike picks up a wrench, crosses to the Datsun on the lift, and starts on the control arm.

IKE
Pack shorts.

EXT. MIDLAND REGIONAL AIRPORT — TARMAC — DAY

A Southwest Airlines 737 sits on blinding concrete. Heat shimmer. It is eighty-one degrees in December.

Down the airstair come the Koskis. All three in full Michigan winter gear: parkas, flannel, Sorel boots, Ike in his Mackinaw. Dean has a Lions stocking cap. They hit the heat like a wall. Stop dead on the stairs.

DEAN
Oh my God.

TOMMY
It's December.

IKE
(already sweating)
I told you to pack shorts.

DEAN
You told us to pack shorts! You didn't say WEAR shorts!

Behind them, an irritated FLIGHT ATTENDANT clears her throat. They shuffle down.

EXT. MIDLAND REGIONAL AIRPORT — CURBSIDE — CONTINUOUS

Waiting at the curb, gleaming: a white 1982 Cadillac Eldorado convertible with a set of six-foot longhorns bolted to the hood. Leaning against it, a DRIVER in a black Stetson and mirrored aviators, the size of a refrigerator, holding a hand-lettered sign: KOSKI (MICHIGAN).

DRIVER
Y'all the Koskis?

IKE
That's us.

DRIVER
Name's Tiny. Mr. Bowie sends his regards and says to tell you, and I quote, "Take off them damn coats before you die of the heatstroke and make me look bad."

INT. / EXT. CADILLAC ELDORADO (MOVING) — DAY

Top down. The flat West Texas landscape unrolls forever: mesquite, caliche, pumpjacks nodding like drinking birds, the sky impossibly large. The three Koskis in the back seat, parkas off, sitting in flannel shirts with the sleeves rolled, pale Michigan forearms glowing like fluorescent tubes. Dean has his stocking cap in his lap like a dead squirrel.

The radio plays George Strait. Tiny drives with one finger.

DEAN
How big's Mr. Bowie's place?

TINY
Ranch is about eighty thousand acres.

DEAN
Eighty thousand?

TINY
Give or take. He lost a couple thousand to a poker game in '79. Won 'em back in '80.

Dean looks at Tommy. Tommy is already doing math on the back of his airline ticket envelope.

TOMMY
(whispering)
That's a hundred and twenty-five square miles.

EXT. ROCKING B RANCH — MAIN GATE — DAY

A massive stone-and-iron gate, a wrought-iron B with a rocker under it. The Eldorado thunders through. A mile of caliche drive, then two. Cattle. A herd of something that is definitely not cattle.

DEAN
Are those... are those zebras?

TINY
Mr. Bowie likes zebras.

EXT. ROCKING B RANCH — MAIN HOUSE — DAY

A sprawling white hacienda with a red-tile roof, a veranda long enough to land a crop duster, a swimming pool shaped like Texas. Three Mercedes, two Ford pickups, a helicopter.

On the veranda steps, arms spread wide, white Stetson, white suit, ostrich boots, bolo tie gleaming: Harlan Bowie. Beside him, CONNIE BOWIE (48), sharp-eyed, blonde, in a turquoise pantsuit, holding a tray of margaritas with the composure of a woman who has run the actual business for twenty years while her husband ran his mouth.

The Cadillac stops. Ike gets out. The two old men look at each other across twenty feet of flagstone.

HARLAN
Eino.

IKE
Harlan.

A beat. Then Harlan comes down the steps and grabs Ike in a hug that lifts him clean off the ground. Nobody has lifted Ike Koski off the ground since the Eisenhower administration.

HARLAN
Lord have mercy, you got old!

IKE
(muffled, being crushed)
You got fat!

HARLAN
(setting him down, delighted)
I got RICH, which is fat with better lawyers!

He turns to the boys. Looks them up and down. Holds out a hand the size of a skillet.

HARLAN (CONT'D)
And these must be the rally drivers. Which one of you bit me?

DEAN
(taking his hand)
That'd be me, sir. I was three.

HARLAN
You drew blood, son. I had a scar for a year. I was proud of it.
(to Tommy)
And you're the brain.

TOMMY
I... how do you know that?

HARLAN
Your daddy sent me a Christmas card in 1968 with a picture of you two on it. You were reading a road atlas. At four years old. Upside down, but you was reading it.

IKE
I thought you said I never sent a card.

HARLAN
You sent ONE card, Ike. In twenty-four years. One.

CONNIE
(stepping in, handing out margaritas)
Welcome to Texas. I'm Connie, I'm his wife, I'm the one who actually signs the checks, and I've heard more about Alberta, Canada in twenty years than any woman should ever have to.

IKE
(taking her hand)
Ma'am.

CONNIE
(warm)
You're the one who pulled him out.

A flicker between Ike and Harlan. Harlan clears his throat, loudly.

HARLAN
Well! Who wants to see my car collection?

EXT. ROCKING B RANCH — BACK PASTURE — AFTERNOON

A dirt course has been scraped into the caliche: a figure-eight around two windmills and a stock tank, a crest over a cattle guard, a long sweeping bend through mesquite. Hay bales mark the edges. Ranch hands in cowboy hats lounge on the fence rails with bottles of Lone Star, watching.

Parked on the course: a CJ-7 Jeep, bright red, no doors, with a whip antenna and a Texas flag.

Harlan stands beside it, holding a crystal tumbler filled to the brim, to the very brim, with bourbon. Meniscus bulging.

HARLAN
Here's how I do business, boys. My daddy taught me: you can tell everything about a man by how he drives when somebody's watchin' and how he drives when he thinks nobody is. Now I'm gonna ride shotgun. Dean, you're gonna drive me around my little course here, fast as you like. And if I spill one drop of this Pappy Van Winkle—

DEAN
No deal?

HARLAN
No deal. And I make you drink what's left.

DEAN
That doesn't sound like a punishment.

HARLAN
You ain't tasted it after it's been sloshin' around a Jeep in ninety degrees.

Ike leans against the fence beside Connie, arms crossed.

IKE
(to Connie)
He did this to me in '58.

CONNIE
What happened?

IKE
I spilled.

CONNIE
And?

IKE
He gave me the money anyway. Said he just wanted to see me try.

Connie smiles at him sidelong. She likes this man.

Dean climbs into the Jeep. Harlan hauls himself into the passenger seat, glass held out in front of him like a sacred relic. Tommy leans in on Dean's side.

TOMMY
(low)
Smooth. Everything smooth. No Scandinavian flick. You flick, you lose the bourbon. You lose the bourbon, we're back at the Copper Kettle fixing tractors.

DEAN
I got it.

TOMMY
Dean. Look at me. Slow in, fast out. Weight transfer. Pretend there's an egg on the gas pedal.

DEAN
(grinning)
You want to drive, Professor?

Just a joke. But Tommy hesitates a half-second too long before he steps back.

TOMMY
Just don't spill.

Dean fires up the Jeep. Revs it. Harlan whoops.

HARLAN
Let 'er buck!

The Jeep launches. Dust explodes behind it. The ranch hands cheer.

EXT. ROCKING B RANCH — DIRT COURSE — CONTINUOUS

Dean takes the first bend and, for about one second, we think he's going to drive like himself. Then something clicks. He brakes early, turns in gentle, catches the slide before it even starts. The Jeep drifts, but the drift is butter. Harlan's glass barely trembles.

HARLAN
Well, I'll be damned.

Down the straight. Faster. The crest over the cattle guard. Dean lifts, just slightly, and the Jeep floats, lands soft as a feather. The meniscus holds.

HARLAN (CONT'D)
(laughing)
Son of a bitch! Son of a bitch, look at that! Not a drop!

Around the stock tank. A cow wanders onto the course. Dean glides around it without breaking the arc. The cow doesn't even look up.

At the fence, Tommy's lips are moving: he's calling the notes silently, to himself, from a hundred yards away. Ike notices. Says nothing.

The Jeep swings through the last bend, the long sweeper through mesquite, and Dean, feeling it now, can't help himself: he lets the tail step out a little further, a little further, a big, beautiful, lurid slide throwing a wall of caliche dust over the fence and the ranch hands and Connie Bowie's turquoise pantsuit—

—and just as the Jeep snaps back straight, Harlan throws his head back and drinks the entire tumbler of bourbon in one gulp.

The Jeep skids to a stop at the fence. The dust drifts. Harlan holds up the glass. Empty. Bone dry.

HARLAN (CONT'D)
(hoarse, triumphant)
Didn't spill a drop!

DEAN
You drank it!

HARLAN
Ain't spillin' if it goes in the right hole!

The ranch hands ROAR. Dean bangs his head against the steering wheel, laughing. Tommy lets out a breath he's been holding for three minutes. Connie wipes dust off her face with a handkerchief.

CONNIE
(to Ike, dry)
Twenty-four years I've been married to that.

IKE
My condolences.

CONNIE
Oh, don't. I love him to death. I just wish he'd die a little quieter.

INT. ROCKING B RANCH — DINING ROOM — NIGHT

A table for twenty set for five. Mounted heads on every wall: elk, bison, bighorn sheep, one zebra (retired). A chandelier made of antlers. The plates are heaped with chicken-fried steak, brisket, jalapeño cornbread, pinto beans, and a salad that nobody touches. Iced tea in pitchers. Lone Star on ice.

The boys eat like men who have lived on pasties and Hamburger Helper for a decade. Harlan watches them with a satisfied host's grin.

HARLAN
So. Talk to me. Tommy. You're the numbers man. Give me your numbers.

Tommy puts his fork down. Wipes his mouth. Pulls out the notebook.

TOMMY
The WRC season is twelve rounds. We're proposing ten. Monte Carlo, Sweden, Portugal, Safari in Kenya—

HARLAN
Africa!

TOMMY
—Corsica, Acropolis in Greece, New Zealand, Argentina, Finland, San Remo in Italy, and the RAC in Britain. We'd skip Ivory Coast and one other, probably New Zealand or Argentina, for shipping. That's three hundred forty thousand, U.S., assuming the pound and the franc behave, which they won't.

HARLAN
And that gets you what, exactly?

TOMMY
A competitive privateer car. Spares. Shipping. Entries. A service truck. Two recce cars. A small crew. Hotels, food. Tires. Lots of tires.

HARLAN
And what's it get me?

Tommy blinks. He hadn't expected the question put that way.

TOMMY
Uh. Exposure. Television in Europe. The Monte's broadcast in fifteen countries. The RAC gets millions of spectators in the forests. Your logo on the car, the suits, the truck—

HARLAN
(waving a rib)
Son, I don't sell oil in Europe. I sell oil to people who sell oil in Europe. My name on a car in France don't do a damn thing for my bottom line.

The boys look at each other. Dean stops chewing.

DEAN
Then... why?

Harlan sets down the rib. Wipes his hands. Leans back.

HARLAN
You know why Texans do things, Dean?

DEAN
No, sir.

HARLAN
Because somebody told 'em they couldn't.
(beat)
Every time I go to London on business, some pinstripe sumbitch with a face like a boiled ham looks down his nose at me like I just walked in off a cattle drive. Which I did, twice, but that's not the point. The point is, they think we're rubes. Americans. They think we can drive in a straight line on the interstate and that's about it. Well. I'd like to see some Frenchman's face when a Texas-sponsored car from Michigan beats him on his own damn mountain.

IKE
(dry)
That, and you're bored.

HARLAN
(pointing at Ike)
And I'm bored! He knows me! Thank God somebody finally knows me!

CONNIE
I know you.

HARLAN
You know me too well, darlin'. That's different.

He leans forward, both elbows on the table.

HARLAN (CONT'D)
Here's my terms. Three things.

IKE
(mutter)
Everybody's got terms.

HARLAN
One. BOWIE OIL, big as it'll fit. On the doors, on the hood, on the roof so the helicopters can read it. And I want a Texas flag somewhere on that car.

TOMMY
We can do that.

HARLAN
Two. I want to be there. Not every rally, I got a business to run. But Monte Carlo, I want to be standin' at the finish in a tuxedo with a bottle of champagne. I have always wanted to go to Monte Carlo and have a reason.

CONNIE
You've been to Monte Carlo four times.

HARLAN
Without a REASON, Connie!

DEAN
And three?

HARLAN
(grinning at Ike)
Three. I want to be Team Principal.

Ike, mid-sip of iced tea, chokes.

IKE
The hell you do.

HARLAN
I'm payin'!

IKE
I'm the team principal. I'm the chief mechanic. I'm their father. I'm the one who says when we pull out. That was in MY terms before you had terms.

HARLAN
Well, what am I, then?

TOMMY
(quick, diplomatic)
Chairman.

Everybody looks at Tommy.

TOMMY (CONT'D)
Chairman of the Board. Bowie Oil Rally Team, Harlan T. Bowie, Chairman. It's above Team Principal. Technically.

HARLAN
(considering)
Chairman.
(tasting it)
Chairman Bowie.
(delighted)
I like that! I like that a whole lot! Connie, get me stationery!

IKE
(muttering, to Tommy)
Technically above?

TOMMY
(muttering back)
Technically there's no such thing.

Harlan stands up. Holds out his hand across the table to Ike.

HARLAN
Three hundred and forty thousand. Plus fifty for when things break, 'cause your boy's right, things break. Wire transfer Monday to whatever bank in Michigan you got.

Ike stands. Looks at the hand. The whole table goes quiet.

IKE
That's too much, Harlan.

HARLAN
It ain't near enough, Ike.

They shake. Long. Two old men holding onto each other a second longer than a business deal requires.

Dean lets out a war whoop. Tommy sits very still, staring at his notebook, where he has already written, in neat pencil: +$390,000. Beneath it, very small: Holy wah.

EXT. ROCKING B RANCH — VERANDA — NIGHT

Late. Crickets. The stars are stupid out here: a million of them, a smear of Milky Way across the whole sky. The boys have gone to bed. Somewhere a coyote. The pool glows turquoise, the shape of Texas.

Ike and Harlan sit in two rocking chairs with a bottle of the good bourbon between them and two glasses, filled to reasonable levels.

A long, comfortable silence.

HARLAN
They're good boys.

IKE
They're good boys.

HARLAN
The little one. Tommy. He was callin' the whole course from the fence. You see that?

IKE
I saw it.

HARLAN
He wants to drive.

IKE
(beat)
I know.

HARLAN
Does Dean know?

Dean doesn't know what he had for breakfast.

Harlan laughs softly. Rocks. Sips.

HARLAN
You never told 'em. About Alberta.

IKE
No need.

HARLAN
There's every need, Ike. You pulled me outta that car. It was on fire and you went back into it. With your bare hands. I still got the—
(touches his own collarbone)
You still got the—
(nods at Ike's left hand)

Ike looks at his left hand. In the porch light, for the first time, we notice: the skin across the back of it is shiny and tight. An old burn scar, long healed.

IKE
You'd have done the same.

HARLAN
(quiet, fierce)
No. No, I wouldn't have. That's the thing. That's what I been carryin' around for eighteen years. I wouldn't have. I'd have stood there on that road and watched you burn and I'd have been sorry about it the rest of my life.

Ike doesn't argue. He just rocks.

HARLAN (CONT'D)
And Walt—

IKE
(sharp)
Harlan.

Harlan stops. A long moment. Whatever "Walt" means, it hangs there in the Texas night between them.

IKE (CONT'D)
(softer)
Some things stay in Alberta.

HARLAN
(nodding slowly)
Some things stay in Alberta.

He refills both glasses.

HARLAN (CONT'D)
So. You got money. What're they gonna drive?

Ike opens his mouth. Closes it. Looks at Harlan.

IKE
(slowly)
I have no idea.

HARLAN
(delighted)
Well, hell, Ike. That seems like somethin' you'd want to figure out.

The coyote howls. Harlan howls back. Ike, after a moment, laughs.

ACT THREE

INT. KOSKI SERVICE & REPAIR — OFFICE — DAY

Tommy sits at the desk, phone jammed between ear and shoulder, writing furiously. Dean paces. Ike leans in the doorway eating a pasty out of wax paper.

Aw, come on.

DEAN
And then they DIED, Tommy! That's the famous part about dinosaurs!

IKE
(wandering out)
I'm gonna go change some oil.

INT. KOSKI SERVICE & REPAIR — BAY ONE — LATE NIGHT

SUPER: "DECEMBER 11, 1982. 42 DAYS TO MONTE CARLO."

Snow blows sideways past the high windows. The Datsun, repaired now, sits on the floor. Ike's gone home. Just the brothers. A space heater glows orange. Dean sits on the Datsun's hood drinking a Stroh's. Tommy stands in front of him holding an Autosport open to a photo: the Renault 5 Turbo, fat-hipped, scoops on its flanks like gills, driven sideways up a mountain road by a small Frenchman with a huge grin.

TOMMY
Renault 5 Turbo.

Dean stares at it. Stares at Tommy. Bursts out laughing.

DEAN
A Renault. A RENAULT. Tommy, Mrs. Kangas's niece in Marquette has a Renault. The Le Car! It's got a canvas roof and it's the color of a Band-Aid!

TOMMY
This isn't a Le Car.

DEAN
It's a Le Car with a gland problem!

TOMMY
Dean, listen to me. It's a completely different car. They took the engine out of the front and put it BEHIND the seats. Mid-engine. Turbocharged. Rear-wheel drive. About two hundred and fifty horsepower in Group 4 trim, maybe more, in something that weighs less than Loretta.
(flips page)
Ragnotti won Monte Carlo in one in '81. Won Corsica with it last year. On tarmac it'll run with anything. The Quattro's a truck on tarmac. It understeers like a shopping cart.

DEAN
And on gravel?

TOMMY
(honest)
On gravel it's a handful. On snow it's worse. It's short, it's twitchy, all the weight's in the back, and the turbo lag is supposedly biblical. You put your foot down, nothing, nothing, nothing, and then it tries to kill you.

DEAN
(slowly)
...So it's rear-wheel drive.

TOMMY
We've driven rear-wheel drive our whole lives. You learned on Dad's Falcon. We know how to drive a car that wants to swap ends. Everyone else in Europe's learning four-wheel drive from scratch this year. We already know how to drive the dinosaur.

DEAN
(stung)
You want to drive the dinosaur because you can't get the future.

TOMMY
I want to drive the car we can BUY, Dean!

DEAN
It's a French hatchback! We're gonna show up to Monte Carlo in a French hatchback and get beat by the French in their own hatchback! You know what that's like? That's like showin' up to the Kentucky Derby on a horse you bought from the Kentuckians!

TOMMY
That's EXACTLY how you win the Kentucky Derby! Everybody buys horses in Kentucky!

DEAN
(hopping off the hood)
You're just scared of the Quattro.

TOMMY
I'm not scared of anything! I'm scared of YOU! I'm scared of you in a Quattro, going a hundred and forty on a road you don't know, because you think four-wheel drive means you can't crash! And I'm scared of the bank! And the pound! And the franc! And Greek customs! And whether Dad's gonna have a heart attack in some parking lot in Portugal!

DEAN
Oh, here we go.

TOMMY
Here we go what?

DEAN
Here we go with Tommy carrying the whole world on his skinny little shoulders again. Poor Tommy. Tommy does everything. Tommy's so responsible. Tommy called Italy.

TOMMY
Somebody had to call Italy!

DEAN
And Tommy's always right! You're always right, and you're always so goddamn CALM about it! You know how annoying that is? Sitting next to you in the car, you readin' your little notes like a librarian, like none of it scares you—

TOMMY
(exploding)
It ALL scares me! Every single corner! Every one! You think I'm calm? I'm calm because one of us has to be! You drive like you're trying to prove something to a guy who isn't there anymore!

That lands like a slap. Dean goes very still.

DEAN
(low)
What's that supposed to mean?

TOMMY
It means Dad's Hump, Dean. It means going flat over the exact spot where he crashed so you could prove you're better than him. It means every time you take a stupid risk, I'm sitting there wondering if it's for us or for you.

DEAN
At least I take the risk! At least I'm the one with his hands on the wheel! You sit there with your pencil—

TOMMY
I'd be faster than you in half those cars!

DEAN
(very quietly)
Say that again.

TOMMY
(backing off)
I didn't— I didn't mean—

DEAN
No. You meant it. You've been meaning it for a while, huh.

TOMMY
Dean...

DEAN
In the Jeep, in Texas. "Pretend there's an egg on the pedal." You were driving it. I felt it. You were driving the whole time from the fence.

DEAN (CONT'D)
(not turning)
You want to drive?

TOMMY
(barely)
Sometimes.

DEAN
...Okay.

The Renault.
